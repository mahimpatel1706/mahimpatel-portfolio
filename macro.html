1. The Setup

The Illusion of 16% Loan Growth: Forgetting Banking 101

Between 2022 and 2024, nearly every consensus research report on Dalal Street told the exact same story: "India’s private banks are entering a multi-year golden era because credit growth is accelerating at 15% to 17% a year."

Large institutions like HDFC Bank, ICICI Bank, and Kotak Mahindra Bank were treated as unassailable compounding machines. But as a student of financial history who spent seven years reading balance sheets, I noticed that almost nobody on TV was asking the most elementary question in commercial banking:

Where is the actual money coming from to fund all these loans?

At its core, commercial banking is not complicated. It is a leveraged spread business. You borrow money cheaply from depositors, and you lend it out at a higher interest rate to borrowers. The spread between what you earn on your loans (Yield on Advances) and what you pay to your depositors (Cost of Funds) is your Net Interest Margin (NIM).

The Arithmetic of Bank Deposits (The Statutory Haircut)

When a saver deposits ₹100 into a bank, the bank cannot simply turn around and lend all ₹100 out:

1. Cash Reserve (CRR)
₹4.50 locked with RBI
Earns exactly 0% return
2. Statutory Liquidity (SLR)
₹18.00 in Govt Bonds
Yields ~6.8%–7.1%
3. Effective Lending Pool
Only ₹77.50 left
For commercial loans

For every ₹100 that walks through the door, only ₹77.50 can actually be deployed into commercial credit. If your loan demand is growing at 16%, but your deposits are only growing at 11%, you are running a structural deficit of 300 to 500 basis points every single month.

2. The Leakage

The Scissors Problem: Where Did Indian Household Deposits Disappear?

Sell-side bank analysts kept insisting that deposit growth would "mean-revert soon." It didn't. Why? Because Indian household savings were undergoing a permanent, once-in-a-generation structural migration.

For decades, Indian families left their surplus money in a bank savings account earning 3.0% to 3.5%, or in a 3-year Fixed Deposit. But post-2020, three powerful forces permanently broke this habit:

  • The Mutual Fund SIP Tsunami: With smartphone apps, UPI, and financial awareness, monthly Systematic Investment Plans (SIPs) surged from ₹8,000 Cr in 2020 to over ₹25,000 Crore every single month by 2025/2026. Over ₹3 Lakh Crore a year that previously sat on bank balance sheets as cheap deposits was now flowing straight into equities and mutual funds.
  • Demat Accounts Quadrupled: Active Demat accounts in India jumped from 4 Crore in 2020 to over 16 Crore. Retail investors realized that keeping money in a 3% savings account meant losing purchasing power to inflation after taxes.
  • The Death of Cheap CASA: Current Account & Savings Account (CASA) balances are a bank’s most profitable funding because they cost almost nothing (~2.8% blended). But depositors got smarter. They swept their idle cash into Gold ETFs, liquid funds, or higher-yielding instruments.

The result was brutal for private giants: HDFC Bank's CASA ratio dropped from ~48% down to ~35%–37% post-merger. Kotak Mahindra Bank saw its industry-leading CASA ratio collapse from historical highs of 60% down to the mid-40s. The cheap money was gone.

3. The Panic

The Loan-to-Deposit (LDR) Trap & The Cost of Funds Surge

When a bank wants to keep lending at 16% but can only gather deposits at 11%, it has only two choices—and both are dangerous:

First, it stretches its balance sheet. Banks pushed their Loan-to-Deposit Ratio (LDR) into territory that makes conservative risk officers nervous. While a safe, prudent LDR is between 70% and 75%, several large private banks saw their LDR shoot past 88% to 92%, with HDFC Bank peaking near ~105%+ post-merger. At that level, the bank has zero margin for error; it is completely dry on spare liquidity.

Second, it starts an expensive price war for deposits. Desperate for cash to balance their books, large banks started bidding aggressively against each other:

• They jacked up 1–3 year retail Fixed Deposit rates to 7.25%–7.75% (and 8%+ for seniors).
• They borrowed heavily in wholesale markets by issuing short-term Certificates of Deposit (CDs) at 7.5%–7.8%.
• The Result: Industry-wide Cost of Deposits climbed by 160 to 220 basis points. The floor beneath their funding costs had permanently shifted higher.
4. The Regulatory Trap

The EBLR Asymmetry: Why Large Banks Couldn't Pass on the Costs

An outsider might ask: "If banks are paying 2% more for deposits, why can't they just charge borrowers 2% more on their loans?"

Because of a crucial regulatory rule that the RBI introduced in October 2019: the External Benchmark Lending Rate (EBLR).

Under RBI rules, all floating retail loans (home loans, personal loans, car loans) and MSME loans must be linked directly to an external benchmark—which is almost always the RBI Repo Rate. Today, more than 60% of private bank loan books are linked to this mechanism.

This creates a trap when the interest rate cycle turns:

The Repricing Asymmetry Trap

When the RBI pauses or cuts interest rates, the interest a bank collects on its loans drops instantly (within 24 to 48 hours). But on the liability side, the bank is legally locked into paying 7.5% on Fixed Deposits that savers locked in for the next two to three years!

Lending yields fall immediately; deposit costs stay stubbornly high. Net Interest Margins (NIMs) were mathematically guaranteed to compress by 30 to 50 basis points.

Then the RBI added more pressure: in late 2023, Governor Shaktikanta Das hiked regulatory risk weights on unsecured personal loans by 25 percentage points. This forced banks to back their riskiest consumer loans with more expensive equity capital, lowering their Return on Equity (ROE).

When a bank's ROE drops from 18% to 14%, its valuation multiple cannot stay at 3.5x Book Value. It de-rates toward 2.0x Book Value. That single dynamic explains why giant private bank stocks went sideways for over two years.

Unlike metro millennials in Mumbai or Bengaluru who switch banks on their phone for an extra 0.25% interest, TMB’s depositors are local merchants and multi-generational business families. They keep their operational current accounts and savings accounts with TMB because of 50-year-old family relationships, daily cash handling, and local trade trust. TMB’s deposit base is granular, loyal, and practically un-shakeable.

It was never forced to issue panic wholesale Certificates of Deposit (CDs), allowing Cost of Deposits to peak at just 5.62% in FY26 before beginning downward repricing.

TMB sits on an astonishing Capital Adequacy Ratio of 32.33% in FY26 (up from 26.15% in FY24)—almost entirely pure Tier-1 equity capital (30.8%). This means TMB has enough capital to fund 5+ years of 18–20% loan growth and absorb virtually any macroeconomic shock without ever needing to dilute its shareholders.

04. Tangible Secured Lending (46.4% Gold Loans @ 53% LTV & 94.7% RAM)

Avoiding the Unsecured Consumer Credit Trap

While large private banks grew aggressively in unsecured personal loans and credit cards (which the RBI eventually penalized), TMB’s loan book remained 94.66% anchored in secured MSME lending (28.2%), agriculture (14.1%), and Gold Loans (46.44%), with unsecured retail at just 0.10%. This is where the math closed the loop. While large banks were priced at 2.5x to 3.5x Book Value and regional peers like Karur Vysya Bank (2.11x P/B) and City Union Bank (1.99x P/B) traded near 2.0x, TMB trades at just 1.35x TTM P/B (1.20x FY27E P/B) and 9.5x TTM P/E (8.4x FY27E P/E), while delivering a superior Return on Assets (RoA) of 2.14% in Q1 FY27 (1.93% in FY26). In investing, real margin of safety is not just a low P/E number. It is finding a business whose balance sheet is so well-funded, and whose customer base is so loyal, that it can comfortably ignore the panic shaking its giant competitors. That is why I invested in Tamilnad Mercantile Bank.

Executive Abstract for CIOs, Fund Managers & Principal Officers

For three decades, the Indian IT services industry has been celebrated as the crowning jewel of domestic capitalism. Yet, when evaluated without sentimentality, its foundation rested entirely on a single economic lever: geographic labor cost arbitrage. Indian IT never sold proprietary software products or foundational compute architectures; it rented billable human hours. Today, the meteoric rise of autonomous agentic systems—such as Claude 3.7, Antigravity, and frontier reasoning harnesses—demonstrates that the routine cognitive tasks of software engineering are being automated at token speed.

The prevailing consensus defense—pointing to fortress cash reserves, calling AI an over-hyped bubble, or hiding behind enterprise privacy restrictions—repeats the fatal complacency of Nokia and Motorola in 2007. The uncomfortable truth is that Indian IT services are currently living on life support provided solely by current silicon bottlenecks: memory bandwidth limitations and inference energy costs. As semiconductor architectures evolve and specialized inference innovators (such as Recursive AI and custom ASIC fabrics) permanently compress token costs below the offshore billable hour, the legacy time-and-materials model will face an irreversible terminal valuation reckoning.

I. The Origin of the Machine

The Thirty-Year Miracle of Human Leverage

To understand the vulnerability of the Indian IT services complex today, one must first respect what it accomplished. In the late 1980s and 1990s, visionaries like F.C. Kohli at Tata Consultancy Services and N.R. Narayana Murthy at Infosys engineered one of the most audacious trade models in emerging-market history: the Global Delivery Model (GDM).

The mechanics were intoxicatingly simple and massively lucrative. A Fortune 500 bank in New York or an insurer in London paid between $60 and $120 per billable hour for application maintenance, bug patching, database migration, and ERP implementations. The Indian service provider delivered that work from Bengaluru, Pune, or Hyderabad, employing engineering graduates at ₹3.5 to ₹5.5 Lakhs per annum (roughly $4,000 to $6,500 annually).

The difference between US Dollar revenues and Indian Rupee wage costs generated operating margins above 25%, funded sprawling campus real estate, and created a corporate aristocracy. It anchored the Nifty 50, provided millions of upwardly mobile middle-class livelihoods, and placed India firmly on the global enterprise technology map.

Yet, beneath this staggering commercial triumph lay an unaddressed structural flaw: this was human leverage, not intellectual property leverage.

When an enterprise scales on human labor, every incremental $50,000 of billing requires recruiting, training, seating, and managing another human being. For thirty years, India’s demographic dividend made this equation work. But it also bred an organizational complacency: Indian IT never learned how to create software products; it only mastered how to bill human time.

II. Capital Allocation as a Confession

The Missed Sovereign Window: Buybacks Over Foundations

The arrival of the transformer architecture in 2017 (*"Attention Is All You Need"*) was not a guarded military secret. It was published in the open academic domain. Over the ensuing seven years, the technological trajectory was unmistakable to anyone paying attention to computing history.

Indian IT leaders had hundreds of billions of rupees in unencumbered free cash flow, zero debt, access to hundreds of thousands of STEM graduates, and intimate, board-level relationships with nearly every Fortune 500 Chief Information Officer on earth.

Did they invest that capital into developing indigenous, sovereign foundational models? Did they pool resources to build specialized silicon architectures, enterprise reasoning compilers, or domain-specific vertical agentic platforms for banking and logistics?

They did almost none of it.

Instead, their capital allocation strategy looked like a textbook case of an aging utility:

  • Paucity of True R&D: While global technology leaders regularly reinvest between 14% and 24% of annual revenues into foundational research and development, Indian IT services firms have consistently reported R&D expenditures hovering around an anemic 0.5% to 1.2% of turnover.
  • Financial Engineering over Technical Boldness: Rather than risking capital on long-gestation AI research or high-risk GPU clusters, Indian IT leadership funneled over 80% to 90% of annual free cash flows directly into share buybacks and dividend distributions. This protected return on equity (ROE) optically on sell-side spreadsheets, but starved the companies of intellectual vitality.
  • The PR Re-badging Exercise: When ChatGPT exploded into the cultural mainstream in late 2022, the corporate response was not structural transformation, but marketing reinvention. Companies announced they had "trained 100,000 engineers in GenAI basics" within six months. But teaching junior engineers how to prompt a third-party commercial API is not an R&D moat—it is an admission that someone else owns the intellectual property and you are merely an operator.
III. The Balance Sheet Illusion

Why Cash Did Not Save Nokia, Motorola, or Kodak

Whenever I present this structural thesis to senior institutional asset managers or equity research analysts, the immediate rebuttal is almost reflexive:

"Look at their balance sheets! TCS holds over ₹40,000 Crore in net cash and investments. Infosys has over ₹15,000 Crore. They have zero debt, generate 20%+ operating margins, and distribute massive dividends. A company with this much cash cannot simply disappear."

This rebuttal confuses financial liquidity with economic survivability. Financial history is a graveyard of cash-rich monopolies that evaporated because they mistook a balance sheet for a moat.

NOKIA (2007) Pre-Disruption Fortress

In 2007, Nokia commanded a staggering 40.4% global mobile handset market share, held over €10.2 billion in net cash, and operated the planet's most formidable hardware distribution channel. Four years after Steve Jobs introduced the iPhone, Nokia’s mobile operating profit had plummeted by over 90%, culminating in a distress sale to Microsoft. Cash could not rewrite Symbian into iOS.

MOTOROLA & BLACKBERRY (2008) Enterprise Lock-in Mirage

BlackBerry (Research In Motion) owned Wall Street, global banking, and government communications. They held zero debt, pristine enterprise SLAs, and billions in cash. Their management believed enterprise security compliance would permanently insulate them from touchscreen consumer devices. Within 60 months, their enterprise moat was zero.

When a fundamental platform shift strikes, cash on the balance sheet is merely a shock absorber that funds a slower, more agonizing decline. It buys time, but it cannot purchase organizational DNA.

IV. The Hardware Moat

Living on Borrowed Silicon: The GPU, Memory Wall & Energy Moat

If agentic coding systems are already capable of generating, debugging, and maintaining software, why hasn't Indian IT collapsed already? Why are TCS and Infosys still reporting positive revenue numbers in FY25/26?

The answer is that Indian IT is currently being kept alive by the physical limitations of artificial intelligence hardware.

Today, deploying an autonomous multi-agent swarm to inspect, refactor, and maintain an enterprise codebase containing 10 million lines of legacy Java or COBOL requires staggering amounts of computational throughput. That throughput runs headlong into three physical walls:

  1. The Memory Wall & HBM Bottlenecks: Modern large language models during inference are fundamentally memory-bandwidth bound, not compute bound. Shuffling model weights across High Bandwidth Memory (HBM3e/HBM4) to process massive enterprise context windows consumes massive energy and creates latency delays.
  2. Datacenter Energy Density & Power Grid Caps: Frontier datacenter clusters require 50 to 100 megawatts of dedicated electrical substation capacity. In North America and Europe, grid interconnection queues stretch 4 to 7 years. Power is currently a hard physical bottleneck limiting the aggregate supply of instantaneous inference tokens.
  3. GPU Capital Expenditure & Token Pricing: Because NVIDIA’s H100 and Blackwell architectures command gross margins above 75%, the cost per million tokens of frontier reasoning remains high enough that paying an offshore human developer in Pune ₹50,000 a month still looks optically competitive for messy, unstructured tasks.
V. The Inevitability of Silicon Advancements

Breaking the Bottleneck: Why the Hardware Shield Will Dissolve

Every single physics and engineering bottleneck currently protecting legacy IT services is being attacked by tens of billions of dollars in silicon research:

  • Custom Enterprise ASICs: Breakthrough chip-design firms like Recursive AI and Groq, alongside hyperscaler custom silicon (Google TPU v6/v7, AWS Trainium), are bypassing generalized graphics pipelines with specialized linear-algebra interconnects, driving inference energy efficiency up by 10x to 30x.
  • In-Memory Computing & Photonic Fabrics: Next-generation packaging (CoWoS, hybrid bonding, CXL pooling) is directly solving the Von Neumann memory wall.

VI. The "AI Bubble" Fallacy

Platforms May Restructure, but Capabilities Do Not Deflate

In March 2000, the Dot-Com bubble burst violently. Hundreds of speculative internet startups went bankrupt; Cisco Systems lost 85% of its market capitalization; trillions in equity evaporated.

Did the internet go away? Did email stop functioning? Did e-commerce die?

Of course not. What happened was that the speculative froth blew off, while the thousands of miles of dark fiber optic cables laid across oceans during the bubble created the hyper-low-cost plumbing that enabled Amazon Web Services, Google, YouTube, and Netflix to conquer the world over the subsequent two decades.

Even if OpenAI or Anthropic experience down-rounds, open-weight architectures (Meta’s Llama, DeepSeek, Mistral, Qwen) have decentralized the intelligence layer. You cannot un-invent automated code synthesis. The capabilities will never roll backward.

VII. The Enterprise Governance Myth

Why the Enterprise Privacy & Maintenance Defense Will Dissolve

In 2010, the consensus among enterprise CIOs was that no serious financial institution would ever host customer account databases on Amazon Web Services or Microsoft Azure because "the public cloud is not secure." Within a decade, JPMorgan Chase, Goldman Sachs, and the CIA were among the largest enterprise cloud spenders on earth.

Air-gapped on-premises Small Language Models (SLMs) and deterministic Abstract Syntax Tree (AST) validation engines provide mathematical auditability that is far superior to human offshore contractors with VPN credentials.

VIII. The Terminal Value Reckoning

The Equity Analyst’s Lens: The DCF Multiple Trap

Consensus sell-side equity research currently values Tier-1 Indian IT companies at forward P/E multiples of 24x to 30x, assuming perpetual terminal growth rates of 4% to 5%.

When volume turns negative in a business with rigid human compensation structures and massive bench costs, operating margins will not stay at 25%. They will compress rapidly into the mid-teens or single digits.

Once institutional markets realize that Indian IT services is not a perpetual growth machine, but a secularly challenged, shrinking legacy annuity with zero pricing power, the sector’s valuation multiples will de-rate from 28x P/E to the 10x–12x P/E multiples historically reserved for cyclical utilities or printing presses. That multiple compression represents the potential destruction of over $150 Billion in equity value across the Indian market.

IX. Epilogue

A Student’s Perspective: What Indian IT Must Do to Survive

To survive, Indian IT giants must execute an urgent, ruthless strategic pivot: proactively cannibalize their own billable-hour revenues with autonomous agent swarms, reinvest cash into sovereign compute clusters and silicon co-design (with innovators like Recursive AI), and transition from headcount brokers into mission-critical system fiduciaries.

The era of labor arbitrage is over. The era of compute parity has arrived.

1. The Macro Paradox

The Illusion of Safety: Corporate Cash Drag and Structural Rupee Debasement

Why would any operating corporation—responsible to public shareholders, board committees, and statutory auditors—ever consider an asset as notoriously volatile as Bitcoin for its balance sheet treasury?

To understand the institutional logic, one must first dismantle the prevailing orthodoxy of corporate treasury management. In standard Indian boardrooms, holding ₹300 Crore to ₹1,000 Crore in short-term Bank Fixed Deposits or AAA Liquid Debt Funds is viewed as the epitome of fiduciary prudence.

In an era of structural currency debasement and accelerating technological inflation, holding idle cash is not "risk-free." It is a guaranteed, slow-motion destruction of corporate purchasing power.

A. The Indian Rupee Structural Depreciation Curve (30-Year Forensic Track Record)

This multi-decade depreciation is not an accident or a temporary policy error. It is structural: driven by India's structural retail inflation differential (~4.5%–6.0% CPI vs. ~2.0%–2.5% in developed markets), an ongoing merchandise trade deficit, and sovereign central bank reserve accumulation policies under classical Purchasing Power Parity (PPP) dynamics.

B. The Corporate Cash Drag Equation: The Math of Melting Ice Cubes

Let us quantify the actual economic reality for an Indian mid-cap IT exporter, specialty chemicals manufacturer, or engineering company holding a ₹300 Crore surplus treasury:

• Corporate Tax Friction: Under Section 115BAA, corporate tax is 22% base + 10% surcharge + 4% cess = effective 25.17%.
• Net Post-Tax Return: 7.00% × (1 - 0.2517) = ~5.24% net annualized yield.

Over a five-year planning horizon, a ₹300 Crore treasury that earns ~5.2% post-tax loses over 17% to 22% of its real purchasing power against the actual operational inputs required to run the enterprise. Wall 1: The Punitive Tax Regime (Finance Act, 2022)

Under Sections 115BBH and 194S of the Income Tax Act, 1961, the Ministry of Finance designed a deliberately punitive tax regime to deter speculation:

  • Effective ~34.32% Flat Tax: Base 30% plus 4% health & education cess and highest applicable corporate surcharge.
  • Zero Loss Set-Off (Section 115BBH(2)(b)): If an Indian corporate buys Bitcoin and incurs a ₹20 Crore loss, that loss cannot be set off against capital gains from another asset, nor can it offset core operating profits. It is a total, dead-weight loss for tax accounting.
  • Zero Operational Expense Deductions: Under Section 115BBH(2)(a), no cost can be deducted except the raw acquisition price. Institutional custodian storage fees, multi-sig hardware security, insurance policies, and legal advisory fees are entirely non-deductible.
  • 1% Gross TDS (Section 194S): Every sale or portfolio rebalance triggers a mandatory 1% tax deduction on gross turnover, draining operating cash flow.

Wall 2: RBI & FEMA Capital Account Restrictions

Under the Foreign Exchange Management Act (FEMA), 1999:

While Indian retail individuals can remit up to ,000 per financial year under the Liberalised Remittance Scheme (LRS), corporates are legally prohibited from utilizing LRS.

Corporate outward remittances fall under the Overseas Direct Investment (ODI) regulations. The Reserve Bank of India has maintained an unyielding stance: Indian operating companies are strictly prohibited from remitting foreign exchange abroad to acquire speculative portfolio assets or digital tokens on global platforms like Coinbase Prime or FalconX.

Consequently, an Indian corporate would be restricted to domestic, onshore FIU-registered exchanges in INR, which lack deep institutional OTC blocks, qualified institutional custody, and multi-billion-rupee depth.

Wall 3: Companies Act 2013, Schedule III, and Director Fiduciary Liability

Since the MCA notification of March 2021, Schedule III of the Companies Act, 2013 mandates explicit disclosure in audited financial statements of all virtual digital asset transactions, balances held, and any advances taken for trading.

More critically, under Section 166 of the Companies Act, directors carry a statutory fiduciary duty to act in good faith and exercise due and reasonable care. If a listed board allocated ₹50 Crore of corporate surplus into Bitcoin, and the asset experienced a typical 40% cyclical drawdown, institutional Domestic Institutional Investors (DIIs like SBI Mutual Fund, HDFC AMC, ICICI Prudential) and proxy advisory firms (like IiAS, SES, and InGovern) would immediately launch governance campaigns against the board for reckless capital destruction.

Wall 4: The Fatal "NBFC 50-50 Principle" Classification Trap

Perhaps the most devastating statutory roadblock—unknown to most retail commentators—is Section 45-IA of the Reserve Bank of India Act, 1934.

The RBI enforces the strictly audited "Principal Business Test" (The 50-50 Rule):

If an Indian operating company's financial assets constitute more than 50% of its total balance sheet assets, AND more than 50% of its gross income is derived from financial assets, the entity is AUTOMATICALLY classified by law as a Non-Banking Financial Company (NBFC).

Consider what happens to a mid-cap software company with ₹400 Crore in operating assets that acquires ₹150 Crore of Bitcoin. If Bitcoin rallies 3x, its digital assets swell to ₹450 Crore—surpassing 50% of total balance sheet assets—and realized/unrealized gains dwarf software sales revenue.

The RBI would mandate that the company register as an NBFC, meet strict 15% Capital Adequacy (CRAR) ratios, maintain statutory liquidity reserves, and face intense banking supervision. An operating enterprise would find its primary business operations legally paralyzed.
